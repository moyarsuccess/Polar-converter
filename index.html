01

Executive summary

Ten lines, then the honest caveats.

What it is

A Canadian company that looks after what a migrant left behind in Iran: an ageing parent, an empty apartment, a pension nobody is chasing, a title deed, a grave. Trained care managers in Tehran do the work. The customer abroad sees photographs, receipts and reports in a dashboard, in Persian and English.

Why now

Somewhere between six and ten million Iranians live abroad, most of them in high-income countries, and the generation that emigrated in the 1980s and 1990s now has parents in their seventies. The alternative to this service is a cousin doing favours — unschedulable, unauditable, and socially expensive to ask twice.

Why it is buildable

The payer and the recipient sit in different countries. Billing happens entirely in Canada on standard card rails, so the business needs no Iranian payment gateway, no rial pricing and no insurance integration — the three things that stall most Iran-facing consumer ideas.

How it makes money

  • Four subscription tiers, $19 to $175 a month, each with an always-on base plus separate monthly quotas by service class.
  • Overage at a published per-request rate that falls as the tier rises.
  • Property priced per door, not per family — the highest-margin line in the catalogue at roughly 86%.
  • One-off jobs and prepaid credit packs for the large share of buyers who will not subscribe to anything yet.

What has to be true

  • Roughly 15–20% of the diaspora has a parent over 65 still living in Iran. Unvalidated, and the most load-bearing number in the plan.
  • A Tehran care manager costs about $360 a month fully loaded with about 120 usable hours. Every margin figure moves with this.
  • The parent will accept a stranger in the house. Refusal churns the account inside 60 days.
Read this document as a structure to argue with, not a price list to publish. Market figures are order-of-magnitude estimates from public reporting, not commissioned research. Every price here is a proposal awaiting the validation work in section 17.

03

Why the model works structurally

The strongest argument in favour of this business is not the market size. It is the geography of the money.

What this buys you. Revenue never enters the Iranian banking system. No domestic gateway, no rial price list, no insurance integration, no sanctioned counterparty on the receiving end of a customer payment.
What it does not buy you. Paying your own Tehran team through informal channels is an operating-expense problem and manageable. Moving customer money outbound — a landlord's collected rent — is a different legal question with a different answer. Keep them in separate workstreams and separate opinions.

Market

Order-of-magnitude sizing, an honest note on how wide the estimates are, and a reframe that changes the denominator.

Source: Iranian official reporting as cited in the source feasibility draft. The 15–20% share assumption is unvalidated and carries the most weight of any figure in this plan. A field check against it is the cheapest high-value research available.

The reframe. Sizing on "has an elderly parent in Iran" gives one to one-and-a-half million households. Sizing on "has anything left behind in Iran" — a parent, a flat, a pension, a deed, a grave, an unfinished legal file — is closer to four million. Elder care is the emotional wedge that earns trust; property and administration are where the volume and the margin sit.

Segments, in order of how easy they are to reach

The worried child

30–50, parent 70+ in Tehran or a provincial city, visits once every year or two. Buys a subscription. Highest emotional urgency, hardest trust barrier.

The absentee owner

Owns one to three units or inherited land. Wants evidence, not care. Buys per door, churns least, and never calls at 3am.

The paperwork exile

Needs a document, a certificate, a legalised translation, a pension chased. Buys once, urgently, and can be acquired on search intent alone.

The returning visitor

Flies back every year or two. Seasonal, high margin, zero liability, and the cheapest route to a first transaction with a stranger.

The heir

A parent has died; an estate is unresolved across borders. Predictable follow-on from end-of-life service, and the highest value per matter.

The second generation

Born abroad, reads English more comfortably than Persian, inherits the problem and the relationship. The reason every report ships bilingual.

How it gets delivered

Three delivery classes, one protocol, and a boundary that is written into the customer contract on day one.

General

Errands ending in a receipt or a photograph. Any vetted local agent. 20–60 minutes, batchable — several in one neighbourhood cost barely more than one.

Meters as 1 unit.

Semi-special

Scheduled work by the named care manager who knows the family, ending in a written or photographic report that takes real time to produce properly. 2–4 hours.

Meters as 3 units.

Special

Needs a nurse, notary or accountant, or carries genuine liability. The cost is the standby capacity and the insurance behind it, not the hour spent.

Meters as 8 units, or quoted per matter.

The life of a single request

One family, one manager — and a pool for the tasks he cannot take

Every family is assigned to a single named care manager who owns the relationship, writes the reports and is the person the customer telephones. That ownership is the product. But a named person is also a single point of failure: he is in a hospital corridor across the city, the task is in a district he does not cover, it falls on a day he is unavailable, or it needs a class he is not cleared for. Refusing the task is not an option, and reassigning the family is worse than the problem.

So the tool carries an internal task pool. The owning manager posts the task, another manager claims it, and the commission is split between them. The family never sees a change of manager.

StepWhat happensRule that makes it work
1 · PostThe owner posts the task with its class, the time window, the district, the spend authority and a short brief drawn from the family's standing notes.Posting is logged against the owner, not hidden. A task cannot enter the pool without a brief — an unbriefed task is how a handed-off visit turns into a bad report.
2 · OfferIt opens to managers cleared for that class, ranked by district first for twenty minutes, then city-wide.Proximity first protects route batching, which is where the margin on general tasks lives. Special-class tasks never open to the general pool — only to cleared managers or a contracted partner.
3 · ClaimThe first eligible manager to claim takes it, and receives the brief, the address and the spend cap — not the medical file.Time-limited, task-scoped access to the family's record. Data minimisation is a privacy obligation here, not a preference: the recipient is not the customer and never consented to a city-wide audience.
4 · SplitThe taker earns 70% of the task credit. The owner keeps 30% for the brief, the supervision and writing it into the monthly report, and keeps the family retainer in full.The owner is paid for the relationship and the taker for the work. Because the retainer is untouched, offloading does not pay — but taking a task always does.
5 · EscalateA scheduled task unclaimed after 24 hours, or a same-day task after twenty minutes, escalates automatically to the operations lead, who assigns it by phone.An unclaimed task must become someone's problem on a clock, or the pool quietly becomes a place where awkward jobs go to die.
6 · ReportEvidence flows into the same activity log. The customer sees “delivered by Reza, supervised by Maryam” — one owner's name on the report as always.Transparent without being confusing. Complaints, quality score and the report deadline all stay with the owner.

How managers are paid, so the split means something

Two components: a family retainer per assigned family, which pays for the relationship, the reporting and being reachable; and task credits per completed task, weighted exactly like customer metering — 1 for general, 3 for semi-special, 8 for special. A hand-off moves 70% of the task credit and none of the retainer.

Making the unpopular slots claimable

Same-day, evening and out-of-district tasks carry a 1.5× credit multiplier. Without it the pool fills with easy morning errands and the 6pm pharmacy run sits unclaimed until the operations lead has to chase it personally.

The cap that keeps ownership real

Above 30% of a manager's monthly tasks handed off, the account goes to capacity review. This is treated as a staffing signal, not misconduct — a manager consistently over the line is either overloaded or wrongly matched to that family.

Why this is more than an internal convenience. Three things fall out of it. Utilisation rises without hiring, because a manager with a quiet Tuesday can absorb someone else's overflow instead of idling. The key-person risk drops, since every family already has several managers who have worked their file. And it is the cheapest possible way into a new city: list tasks in the pool for vetted independents in Mashhad or Isfahan and watch the fill rate before committing to a salaried manager there. The pool is the first piece of software in this business that genuinely cannot be a spreadsheet, and it belongs in Phase 2.
What to watch for. Two failure modes are predictable. A manager who hands off the emotionally hard visits and keeps the easy errands will show a normal hand-off rate and a falling family satisfaction score — which is why quality is tracked separately for owned and handed-off tasks. And a pool that fills instantly every time means the assignment model is wrong: families are being matched to managers who cannot serve their district.

The operating team

Tehran

  • Care managers — named to families, run visits, write reports, escort appointments. Two minimum before any top-tier account is sold; each covers roughly 120 usable hours a month after travel and admin.
  • General agents — errands, queues, deliveries, batched by neighbourhood and day. Paid per run, fungible by design.
  • Operations lead (from month 6) — rota, quality sampling, partner relationships, escalation.
  • Partners under contract — nursing agency, one or two clinics, contractors, notary, accountant, translator.

Canada

  • Founder, part-time alongside a full-time role — sales, customer relationships, product, the legal and banking structure.
  • Contract counsel — one opinion on sanctions and services, one on handling third-party money. Not optional and not cheap.
  • Bookkeeper from first revenue, because expense pass-through only works if the receipts reconcile.

Key-person risk is the single largest operational exposure: the relationship lives with one care manager and the company lives with one founder. Named backups and documented client files are the only mitigation that works.

Quality control, because the default assumption is that you are skimming

Evidence by default

Timestamped arrival, photographs against a fixed checklist, itemised receipts visible in the dashboard. Nothing is marked done without artefacts.

No cash custody

Official fees and contractor invoices pass through at cost with the receipt attached. Two-agent rule on any high-value transaction. No valuables held, ever.

Coordination only

The platform coordinates care and never provides or interprets it. A care manager reports observations and never says "it's probably nothing" — the moment one does, the liability profile of the whole business changes.

Named, with a named backup

"Your manager is on leave" must never mean "nobody knows your mother". Every file is written so a second manager can take over the same week.

Published escalation

A response-time target per tier, a route past the local agent to the Canadian entity, and a written post-incident report within 24 hours of anything going wrong.

Consent from the recipient

The parent is not the payer, and dignity is not a feature request. Cameras, monitoring and data retention require the elder's documented, informed consent.

Packaging and pricing

Four mechanisms, combined: an always-on base, metered quotas by class, published overage, and modules priced per asset rather than per family.

A flat tier with an implied open scope invites the heaviest users to consume the most, which is exactly backwards once the catalogue is 129 services wide. The fix is not a higher price — it is making consumption explicit and welcome.

1 · The always-on base

What the tier buys unconditionally: the emergency line, the dashboard, the named manager, the reporting rhythm. This is standby capacity, and it costs money whether anyone calls or not.

2 · Separate quotas by class

A monthly allowance of general, semi-special and special requests, on three separate counters. One pooled bucket lets a customer spend the whole allowance on the most expensive class.

3 · Overage at a published rate

Exceeding a quota is normal, and priced. Overage is a feature: it is how a customer discovers for themselves that they belong on the tier above.

4 · Modules priced per asset

Property per door, legal per matter, travel per trip. Bundling those into a family tier destroys the tier's margin and undercharges the heavy asset owner.

The four subscriptions

Changing the shape of a package

Every tier is a starting point, not a box. Eight levers let a customer reshape one, grouped by the question they are actually asking. Each reads the same way: what they say, what happens, and why the rule is written that way.

“Who does this cover?”

Both my parents

+40% of the tier, not double. The visit, the travel and the manager relationship are shared; only the reporting and the medical coordination genuinely double.

Charging full price for a second parent at the same address is the commonest way this category loses a sale.

My mother in Tehran and my aunt in Karaj

+$25 a month per additional household or city.

Covers the travel and the lost route batching when the second address is nowhere near the first.

My brother and I both worry

Up to four payers on one account, each billed their agreed share separately, each with dashboard access.

Turns one $95 decision into three $32 decisions — a materially easier conversation in a family that discusses everything.

“What if we don't use it all — or need more?”

A quiet month

Rollover: unused quota carries one month on Watch, two on Vigil, none on the lower tiers.

Quiet months are normal in elder care. Losing the whole allowance every month reads as a con and drives cancellation.

A heavy month

Overage at a published rate that falls as the tier rises — $8 to $14 for a general request, $18 to $28 for an extra visit. Nothing is refused for being over quota.

Overage is how a customer discovers for themselves that they belong on the tier above, without being sold to.

“I'd rather have three small things”

Swap down: one semi-special may be traded for three general requests, once a month. Never upward, and special events are never swappable.

Real flexibility without letting a $45 customer convert an allowance into partner-delivered work that costs $70 to fulfil.

“Just be there for an afternoon”

Time instead of tasks: any quota can be drawn as plain agent hours — one hour per general unit, three per semi-special. Clinical and partner work excluded.

Some families don't know what they want done; they know they want someone present. Selling time absorbs every request nobody anticipated.

“What am I committing to?”

Pay for the year

Ten months for twelve.

Front-loads hard currency, removes eleven chances for a card to fail, and cuts the number of cross-border transfers you have to make.

“She's coming to stay with us for two months”

Pause at 50% for up to two months instead of cancelling. The file, the vault and the manager stay put.

A temporary absence is the commonest reason accounts close. A pause keeps the relationship that took four months to build.

“What if this isn't right?”

Change tier any month, up or down. Cancel monthly, no notice, no clause.

In a trust-deficit category a lock-in clause is read as a trap. Retention comes from the document vault and the relationship, never the contract.

A worked example. Two siblings in Toronto, both parents in Tehran, one empty apartment in the same district. Watch at $95, second parent at +40% ($133), one door on standing vacant care at $39 — $172 a month, split two ways at $86 each, or $1,720 prepaid for the year instead of $2,064. The quota that buys: two visits, eight check-in calls, six errands, three semi-special requests and one special event a month, with a 90-minute emergency target. Everything sold outside a tier

A subscription is one of three ways to buy, and it is not the first one most people choose. The other two are not extras or upsells — they are the acquisition engine, and together they are expected to carry a fifth of revenue.

1 · Standing modules

Recurring, priced per asset. A flat, a parcel of land, a car, a pension, a grave. Bought monthly like a subscription but attached to a thing rather than a person, so an owner with three flats pays three times.

2 · One-off jobs

Single purchases, priced individually. No commitment, no account required. Roughly 1.6× the in-plan equivalent — the gap is deliberate, because this menu exists to acquire subscribers rather than to compete with them.

3 · Prepaid strips

A balance of credits, six-month validity. For the buyer who wants the service occasionally and refuses any recurring charge. The bridge between one job and a tier.

1 · Standing modules, priced per asset

Never folded into a family tier. An owner with three apartments costs three times as much to serve and values the service three times as highly; bundling property into a care tier undercharges exactly the customers most willing to pay.

2 · One-off jobs, with prices

The complete published menu, grouped by the category each job sits in. Official fees, contractor invoices and gift costs are always passed through at cost with the receipt visible — the platform earns only the stated fee.

The three that do the most work. A gift delivery photographed with the recipient is the referral engine — it costs almost nothing and it produces an image the customer forwards to their siblings. A single welfare visit with a report is the natural trial purchase for a sceptic. And certified translation with legalisation has demand entirely independent of elder care, which makes it the one line worth buying search keywords for before any trust exists.

3 · Prepaid strips, for the people who will not subscribe

Five errands · $85

5 general credits. Saves $15 against one-off pricing.

Three visits · $115

3 semi-special credits — a welfare visit with a report, or an appointment escorted and summarised.

Mixed ten · $190

7 general and 3 semi-special. The natural purchase after one good experience.

Credits spend on any general or semi-special service in the catalogue at the same 1 and 3 unit weights used inside the tiers, or as plain agent hours. Special events are never sold as credits — they are quoted. The conversion mechanic: a customer who spends a strip within 60 days is offered the matching subscription with the unused balance carried across, which is a warm upgrade conversation with evidence already delivered.

Deliberate divergence from the source draft. These tiers run above the $15–20 / $40–60 / $80–120 originally proposed, and there are four rather than three. The reason is not optimism: the source ranges were benchmarked against a comparable service whose tiers did not include metered general and semi-special work. Once agent hours sit inside the tier, the price has to cover them. If validation shows the ceiling really is $60, the correct response is to cut the top tier's quotas, not the price.

Sales and go-to-market

Two people have to say yes, and the second one is not paying.

The structural fact that shapes the whole funnel. The child signs the contract; the parent receives the service. If the parent refuses the visitor — and elderly Iranian parents frequently do, out of pride, privacy, or a refusal to be treated as a burden — the account churns within 60 days no matter how good the product is. Parent acceptance is a pre-sale gate, not an onboarding step, and it is a gate you must be willing to fail.

The funnel, at two very different scales

The same seven steps, once as one person can run them and once with paid channels and a team. The shape barely changes; only the top of it does. Both are planning placeholders, and the step to measure honestly is the parent gate.

StepWhy it is shaped that way
Content before offerThis category does not convert on a direct advertisement. The buyer is not searching for a product; they are carrying a worry they have not named yet. Naming the worry — "the Friday call that tells you nothing" — outperforms feature advertising in comparable trust-sensitive services.

It is right for the paperwork jobs: somebody typing "certified translation Iran documents Toronto" has named their problem, wants it solved this week, and needs no emotional preparation at all. Buy those keywords early and treat the resulting customers as the top of the care funnel six months later.

Unit economics

One assumption drives every number on this page.

The load-bearing assumptions. A Tehran care manager costs roughly $360 a month fully loaded with about 120 usable hours after travel and admin — $3 an hour. Every number below moves proportionally with those two figures, and both need verifying in Phase 0 before any price is published.

The rate card the hours are built from

The source draft's hours column was internally inconsistent — its upper-tier figures implied a semi-special request taking about ninety minutes while the class definition says two to four hours. Everything here is rebuilt from one explicit rate card instead, so the arithmetic can be checked and corrected against real timesheets.

General request

0.5 hr

20–60 minutes including travel, assuming it rides an existing neighbourhood route. An urgent, unbatched general request costs closer to 1.5 hours, which is what the overage price covers.

Semi-special request

2.5 hr

Mid-point of the 2–4 hour class definition, including the time to write the report properly. Report-writing is the part that gets underestimated.

In-person visit

2.0 hr

The standard protocol visit plus travel across a large city. Counted separately from the semi-special quota because every tier includes visits outright.

Special event

3.0 hr

Direct time only. It excludes the standby capacity and the partner invoice, which is exactly why special events are capped or quoted rather than bundled.

Tier base

0.5–3 hr

Check-in calls, being reachable, keeping the file current and producing the report rhythm. Rises with the tier: half an hour on Voice, three on Vigil.

Expected utilisation

55%

Mid-point of the 40–70% target band. Nobody consumes a full quota every month, and that gap is most of the real margin — which is why it is stated as an assumption here rather than hidden inside a margin figure.

The quota is a ceiling, not a forecast — and that is a deliberate bet. Every tier above is priced on the assumption that customers use roughly half of what they are entitled to. The bet is reasonable in this category and it is also the single easiest way for this plan to be wrong. If pilot utilisation comes in at 85% rather than 55%, the correct response is to cut quotas at renewal, not to raise prices on a book you have just acquired. Measure it from the first month.

Three uncomfortable conclusions

Elder care is the wedge, not the business

Property earns a better margin, scales better per agent hour and carries none of the reputational tail risk of a bad medical outcome. Sequencing elder care first is right for trust-building. It is not a judgement about where the profit is.

The top tier is a capacity trap

Four Vigil clients consume one full manager. Sell it before the Tehran team has depth and a single resignation takes out your highest-paying accounts simultaneously. Cap it at 15% of the book.

Margin lives in under-use

The table assumes full quota consumption. Real utilisation in subscription concierge sits well below 100%, and that gap is most of the actual margin — which means utilisation has to be measured before final prices are set.

Is $565k in year three too low?

For the market, yes — obviously. Three hundred families is about 0.02% of the households with a parent still in Iran. Nothing in this plan is limited by how many people want the service. Four things limit it instead, and all four are worth stating plainly because they are also the list of things to attack.

Tehran hiring, above all

A manager carries about ten Watch-equivalent families. Three hundred families needs roughly thirty trained, supervised, named managers in a city with no HR function and one operations lead. Recruiting, training to protocol and quality-sampling that many people is the actual growth rate of this business.

Founder hours

Every care sale involves a twenty-minute assessment call and a Persian conversation with a parent. Sixty paid starts a month needs three people doing nothing else. One part-time founder produces the Phase 2 funnel, not the Phase 4 one.

Trust compounds slowly

Referral-led growth in a category where the incumbent is a cousin runs at roughly 0.3 referrals per retained account per year. That is a good number for this category and a slow one in absolute terms.

No outside capital assumed

Growth is funded from contribution, which caps hiring ahead of demand. This is a choice, not a constraint — but it is the choice this projection is built on.

Legal and compliance structure

Four questions that need answers in writing before the first paid customer, not after.

Sanctions posture

The customer is in Canada, pays a Canadian company in hard currency, and receives a service. That is a different position from prohibited trade, but the answer must come from counsel in plain language, be published as a customer-facing FAQ, and never be improvised in a sales call.

Third-party money

Collecting rent, holding a deposit or moving funds outbound for a customer is where a service coordinator becomes something that needs a licence. The standing policy: no custody of cash, gold or valuables; no discretionary investment; no currency transfer as a standalone product; read-only financial follow-up only.

Privacy of a person who is not the customer

The elder's health observations, photographs and documents belong to them. Canadian privacy law applies to the entity holding them. Consent is obtained from the parent directly, in Persian, and recorded — including for cameras and for reporting to siblings.

Contract stack

Customer terms with the coordination-only boundary and a published SLA; care-manager agreements with confidentiality and conduct rules; partner agreements with the nursing agency, clinics, notary and accountant; and a data-processing appendix for the dashboard.

The single hardest line in the catalogue. Cross-border rent remittance sits at the intersection of all four questions above. It is excluded from the price list on purpose. Opening that legal workstream now, in parallel with the pilot, is what makes the property layer launchable in Phase 3 rather than never.

The next thirty days

Validation costs almost nothing and decides whether anything else in this document is worth building.

Twenty structured interviews

Diaspora adults with a parent in Iran. One question above all: what did you do the last time something went wrong, and what did it cost you in money, hours and favours?

Price sensitivity across all four tiers

A van Westendorp survey inside the same interviews: too cheap, cheap, expensive, too expensive. Four tiers means four passes, and it will probably move at least two of the prices in this document.

A fake-door test on the one-off menu

Publish the just this once menu with prices and a waiting-list button. Measure which of the 129 services people actually click. Expect the ranking to contradict the emotional narrative.

Verify the care-manager cost

Two real conversations in Tehran about what $360 a month buys, and how many hours survive travel and admin. Every margin figure here rests on this one number.

Test the parent gate on five parents

Before selling anything, ask five elderly parents in Iran whether they would accept a weekly visitor arranged by their child. If more than a third refuse, the product needs redesigning and no amount of marketing fixes it.

Build the sample report

One fictional but realistic monthly report, bilingual, with photographs, a checklist, a doctor summary and receipts. It is the landing page, the sales deck and the answer to the theft objection, all at once.

The decision gate. If the interviews show willingness to pay at or above the Visit tier, the parent gate passes above 70%, and the care-manager cost holds within 20% of $360, proceed to the pilot. If any one of the three fails, fix it before spending the €4,000 — not after.

The naming system

One pattern, used everywhere: the sentence the customer was already saying before they found you.

Every category and every service is named in the customer's first person, as a thing they cannot do from where they are. The pattern is The [concrete noun] I can't [physical verb]. It does three jobs at once: it describes the service, it states the problem, and it puts the customer's own words on your price list.

The rules, so anyone can write the next one

Five things a name must do

  • Speak in the first person, singular. Not "families can't", not "we handle".
  • Use a concrete noun you could photograph — a rug, a queue, a cake, a deed, a chair. Never an abstraction like "wellbeing" or "coordination".
  • Use a verb of physical presence — knock, sit, carry, walk, stand, hand over, dress, count.
  • Stay under six words, so it fits on a button and inside a sentence.
  • Be true. If the service does not actually remove that exact inability, the name is a lie and the first delivery exposes it.

Four ways to get it wrong

  • Cuteness over accuracy. "The hug I can't give" sells nothing and promises something you cannot deliver.
  • Repeating verbs. If three categories all say "attend", the set reads as a template rather than a voice.
  • Using it internally. Care managers, protocols and the capacity model use plain operational names. Every service carries both, and the operational one is what appears on a rota.
  • Overuse at the wrong altitude. Nine category names and a one-off menu carry the pattern beautifully. Applying it to invoices, legal terms and error messages turns a voice into a tic.
